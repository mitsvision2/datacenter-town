# Let agent color own hue, position carry stance

The literature offers a clear form for most of these seven data types, but almost all of it was built for populations of hundreds to millions of agents. An 8-agent dashboard should take the simplest per-entity version of each form. **Stance over time** is conventionally drawn as per-agent trajectory ("spaghetti") lines, paired with a current-distribution strip and summarized as mean (bias) and standard deviation (diversity) ([Chuang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09618v4)). **Diffusion** is conventionally drawn as a node-link cascade tree with the seed highlighted, plus a cumulative-reach curve ([Goel et al.](https://www.5harad.com/papers/twiral.pdf); [Generative Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). **Small social networks** read best as node-link diagrams ([Ghoniem et al.](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/ghoniem-fekete-castagliola-acomparisonofthereadabilityofgraphsusingnodelinkandmatrixbasedrepresentations-2004)). **Map views** follow the sprite-town-with-bubbles pattern of Smallville and the "world view + plots + monitors" layout of NetLogo ([NetLogo](https://docs.netlogo.org/interfacetab.html)). **LLM cost** is shown as time series, KPI tiles and ranked bars, grouped by model or feature ([LangSmith](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/dashboards)). The rule that ties these together rests partly on sources and partly on our inference. Eight categorical colors is already the practical ceiling ([Wilke](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/color-pitfalls.html)), so agent identity should be the *only* hue channel in the dashboard. Stance, cascade role, activity and call type must then use position, shape, dash or luminance instead. Two data types have weak precedent: memory streams, which no paper charts as data, and per-agent swimlane timelines, which appear only once (Project Sid). The recommendations for those are mostly our own synthesis, and this report marks them as such throughout.

How to read this report: each section separates **what the sources establish** from **our inference**. "Our inference" covers the research team's judgments about fit for an 8-agent, fixed-color dashboard; no cited source states them.

## Eight hues already spend the whole color budget

Every recommendation below depends on one constraint, so it comes first.

**From the sources.** Wilke advises that qualitative scales work best with 3–5 categories, and at 8–10 "the task of matching colors to categories becomes too burdensome." He recommends **direct labeling once there are more than about eight categories** ([Wilke, ch. 19](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/color-pitfalls.html)). The European Data Portal suggests about 7 as a good maximum ([data.europa.eu](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/colour-for-categories)). Munzner says small, scattered color regions support only 6–12 bins and that color is "surprisingly bad for absolute comparisons" ([Munzner slides](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/547-14/slides/color-stone.pdf)). Cleveland and McGill rank **position on a common scale as the most accurate encoding** and color saturation among the least accurate ([Cornell slides](https://info3312.infosci.cornell.edu/slides/04-graphical-perception.html)). Kornhauser, Wilensky and Rand's ABM visualization guidelines add four points: hue cannot be ordered, so it should not encode quantity; backgrounds should be low-contrast and low-intensity so agents stand out; important variables deserve **redundant encoding**; and figurative icons can impede gestalt perception ([JASSS 12/2/1](https://www.jasss.org/12/2/1.html)).

**Our inference.** The 8 agent colors should mean the same thing on every chart. Stance goes on y-position, never on a diverging hue. Cascade role (seed, direct, secondhand) goes on glyph shape and line dash. Activity or location goes on labels or lightness. LLM call types get a neutral or sequential scheme, never the agent palette. Each agent mark should also carry a name or initial so it does not rely on color alone; this follows Kornhauser's redundancy advice and helps colorblind viewers. The Kornhauser warning conflicts with a pixel-art peg-sprite style. Keeping sprites small, giving each a solid outline or patch in its agent color, and desaturating the town map keeps identity readable. This matters because the field's most-cited LLM opinion figure does the opposite: Chuang et al. color lines by *initial opinion* on a blue-to-red diverging ramp ([arXiv 2311.09618](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09618v4)). Copying that convention would put a second meaning on the hue channel.

## Stance scores: spaghetti lines suit eight agents, not populations

**From the sources.** Opinion-dynamics work uses two main forms. The first is per-agent trajectory lines, with opinion on y and time on x. The second is a distribution view: a final-state histogram, or a "colored histogram" of opinion density over time. Chuang et al.'s LLM opinion simulation pairs **a trajectory panel with a final-distribution panel**, arranges treatment conditions (confirmation bias × framing) as a small-multiples grid next to no-interaction baselines, and summarizes outcomes as bias (the mean) and diversity (the SD) ([arXiv 2311.09618](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09618v4)). The two-panel layout (trajectories on top, current distribution below) is also standard in Hegselmann–Krause and Deffuant teaching tools ([HK explainer](https://rf.mokslasplius.lt/hegselmann-krause-bounded-confidence-model/); [Frankfurt applet](https://itp.uni-frankfurt.de/~gros/StudentProjects/Applets_2014_OpinionDynamics/index.html)). Flache et al.'s review uses distribution evolution to illustrate consensus, clustering and bi-polarization ([JASSS 20/4/2](https://www.jasss.org/20/4/2.html)). Bramson et al. separate polarization into **nine distinct formal measures** ([J. Math. Sociology](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0022250X.2016.1147443)), so "polarization" needs a defined measure before anyone plots it. The fake-news LLM simulation "From Skepticism to Acceptance" pairs aggregate compartment counts with a **micro case study of two named agents' trajectories** ([arXiv 2403.09498](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.09498v2)). That is a direct precedent for following individual named agents.

For transitions, alluvial diagrams show changing group membership over time ([Rosvall & Bergstrom](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0008694)). Slopegraphs show before/after for each entity, but only work for two time points and get busy with many categories ([Wikipedia: Slope chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_chart)). For interventions, interrupted-time-series guidance calls for a **labeled vertical line or shading at the interruption** and a counterfactual trend in a different line pattern ([Turner et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7818488)). In practice, only **17% of 217 reviewed ITS graphs drew the counterfactual**, while 73% marked the interruption ([Gahner](https://erikgahner.dk/?p=23392)). For sparse or irregular samples, the advice is to leave gaps rather than connect across missing readings, and to use step lines so the sampling interval shows ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/academy/patchy-data); [Bocoup](https://bocoup.com/blog/showing-missing-data-in-line-charts)). Some tools default to drawing missing values as zeros, which misleads ([Qlik](https://community.qlik.com/t5/Ideas/Fix-continuous-line-chart-s-display-of-missing-values/idi-p/1729498)). For overplotted lines, the two standard remedies are **highlight-and-dim** and **small multiples on shared axes** ([Python Graph Gallery](https://www.python-graph-gallery.com/124-spaghetti-plot); [data.europa.eu](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/number-of-series)).

**Our inference.** At N=8 the trajectory chart is the right primary form, and the population forms (density heatmaps, ridgelines, alluvials of stance bins) would look sparse and blocky. An alluvial with flows 1–8 units wide mostly shows individual moves, which a plain list or a 3×3 oppose/neutral/support transition table conveys more clearly. The recommended chart has these parts:

- Identity-colored lines with a dot at every actual reading, and breaks where an agent has no reading.
- End-of-line labels or sprites.
- Hover-to-highlight.
- A labeled vertical rule at each whisper injection, plus a per-agent tick at the moment that agent actually received the whisper, since exposure lags injection.
- A dot strip of the current 8 readings in place of Chuang's histogram.
- A small bias/diversity strip.
- A slopegraph or dumbbell for start vs. now, or pre vs. post whisper.

Rationale and evidence fit best as details-on-demand: clicking a dot opens the reading's justification and its cited memories. A true counterfactual needs a control run with the same seed and no whisper. Without one, a before/after comparison describes change but does not support causal claims. One unsourced caution: if LLM scores cluster on round numbers, the 0–100 scale is effectively coarse, and readers should not be invited to over-read 2-point wiggles. Chuang plotted a 5-level classifier output as continuous lines, which is a mild version of the same problem ([arXiv 2311.09618](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09618v4)).

## Whisper diffusion: cascade research draws trees, not timelines

**From the sources.** Cascade papers use two figure families. The first is small **node-link cascade trees with the root highlighted**: seeds as large green points with other nodes colored by depth ([Goel, Watts & Goldstein](https://5harad.com/papers/diffusion.pdf)), the root in red ([Cheng et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.4608)), or the source in orange ([arXiv 2006.01027](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.01027)). The second is aggregate statistics compared across thousands of cascades: log-log CCDFs of size and structural virality, and time-to-reach-depth curves ([Goel et al. 2016](https://www.5harad.com/papers/twiral.pdf); [Vosoughi et al. via Mother Jones](https://adops.motherjones.com/politics/2018/03/new-study-twitter-users-spread-fake-news-farther-and-faster-than-the-truth)). Cheng et al. overlay the cascade as **solid edges on dashed friendship edges**, separating "transmitted" from "connected" ([ar5iv 1403.4608](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.4608)). Goel et al. treat cascades "as static objects representing the final state," so time is absent from the tree drawing. The older diffusion-of-innovations tradition plots the **cumulative adopter S-curve** ([Goel et al. 2016](https://www.5harad.com/papers/twiral.pdf); [arXiv 1906.04206](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.04206)).

In LLM simulations, Generative Agents shows its party-invitation spread as **one who-told-whom path diagram**, with awareness counts given only in text (1 agent, 4%, to 13 agents, 52%) ([arXiv 2304.03442](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). Project Sid's religion-spread figure is the richest precedent. It includes an **event plot of keyword chats per agent marking direct vs. indirect converts**, per-agent conversion level over time, counts per conversion level, and a conversion graph separating critical from non-critical exposure edges ([arXiv 2411.00114](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)). The visualization literature contributes **storyline charts**: time on x, one line per character, with convergence showing co-presence ([xkcd 657](https://xkcd.com/657/); [Tanahashi & Ma](https://ieeevis.org/year/2012/paper/infovis/design-considerations-optimizing-storyline-visualizations)). Their known costs are wiggles, crossings and clutter ([arXiv 2408.01775](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01775v1)). For attributes spreading over a graph, Archambault and Purchase found small multiples faster than animation with no difference in error, and users preferred hierarchical layouts to force-directed ones ([Monash](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/on-the-effective-visualisation-of-dynamic-attribute-cascades/)). None of these sources encodes per-node before/after stance change; when cascade papers use color for an outcome, it marks a whole cascade's class (true vs. false).

**Our inference.** No source names a "time-anchored cascade" as a convention, but it is the best fit here. It is a hybrid of Project Sid's event plot and a storyline:

- 8 fixed lanes, one per agent in its color, with sim time on x.
- A distinct seed glyph (star or diamond) where the whisper is injected directly.
- Curved arrows from teller lane to listener lane at the moment of each secondhand retelling.
- Solid arrows for first exposure and dashed for later re-exposures, because hearing from several people turns the tree into a DAG.

Fixed lanes avoid storyline layout optimization entirely. Stance before/after belongs in a companion dumbbell (one row per agent, hollow dot before, filled dot after), not in node color. Also useful is a cumulative "agents reached" step curve split into direct vs. secondhand. With n=8 it is a staircase, not a smooth S, and should be drawn that way. CCDFs and structural virality only make sense in a multi-run comparison view.

## Who talks to whom: node-link on a fixed circle

**From the sources.** Ghoniem, Fekete and Castagliola found **node-link preferable for small, sparse graphs**. Matrices win once graphs pass about 20 vertices or become dense, except for path-finding tasks ([BibBase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/ghoniem-fekete-castagliola-acomparisonofthereadabilityofgraphsusingnodelinkandmatrixbasedrepresentations-2004); [Fekete slides](https://aviz.fr/~fekete/Visualization%20of%20Graphs.pdf)). Beck et al.'s survey makes **how time is represented** (animation vs. static timeline) the primary design choice for dynamic graphs, ahead of node-link vs. matrix ([Uni Duisburg-Essen](https://vis.informatik.uni-due.de/en/publications/a-taxonomy-and-survey-of-dynamic-graph-visualization-13409)). Small multiples were faster than animation on all five tasks tested, though they produced more errors on questions about co-occurrence ([Archambault et al. 2011](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/animation-small-multiples-and-the-effect-of-mental-map-preservati/)). Edge crossings are the most damaging aesthetic in general, but matter little in small graphs with wide crossing angles ([arXiv 0810.4431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0810.4431)).

The LLM simulation papers draw surprisingly few networks. Generative Agents reports network density rising **from 0.167 to 0.74** as a number, with no graph ([arXiv 2304.03442](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). Project Sid draws a directed relationship graph of 50 agents with red/blue edges for sentiment ([arXiv 2411.00114](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)). OASIS shows a graph of newly formed follow relationships ([arXiv 2411.11581](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11581)). Humanoid Agents charts relationship closeness as a time series ([arXiv 2310.05418](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05418)).

**Our inference.** Place the 8 agents on a **fixed circular layout** in their colors, with edge width encoding conversation count. A deterministic layout keeps positions identical across time steps and runs, which removes the mental-map problem that dominates dynamic-graph evaluation. Never re-run a force layout on update. An 8×8 matrix heatmap with colored agent chips on its rows and columns is a strong alternative if most pairs talk, because the graph is then dense. Show change over time with a time scrubber over the fixed layout or with small multiples per sim day, not with free animation. The most important design choice is to separate the two edge types. **"Talked" edges should be thin and neutral, and "transmitted the whisper" edges emphasized** in a single non-agent accent (Cheng et al.'s solid-on-dashed idea), so the cascade does not drown in ordinary chatter. Selecting an agent should highlight its ego network and fade everything else. A chord diagram looks attractive but loses time and turns into a tangle of 8 colors; at this scale the matrix says the same thing more precisely.

## Town map and activities: keep the sprite view, add a swimlane

**From the sources.** The Smallville UI is the genre's reference. It shows an overhead Phaser sprite town with **an emoji speech bubble over each agent** for its current action, click-through to the full action text, and separate live, replay and demo routes ([arXiv 2304.03442](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442); [GitHub](https://github.com/joonspk-research/generative_agents)). It has no time axis: no per-agent timeline, no location heatmap. AgentSociety's web UI replays movement trajectories, state changes, "inner activities" and dialogues, and opens a detail panel when an avatar is clicked ([AgentSociety docs](https://agentsociety.readthedocs.io/en/latest/01-get-started/04-webui.html)). Its spatial result figure is a regional activity-level map ([arXiv 2502.08691](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08691)). Project Sid has the only **per-agent role timeline (swimlane)** among the papers checked, plus a spatial scatter of agents colored by an attribute ([arXiv 2411.00114](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)). On the ABM side, NetLogo and Mesa establish the **world view + plots + monitors + controls** layout, and NetLogo's scrolling Output widget serves as the event log ([NetLogo](https://docs.netlogo.org/interfacetab.html); [Mesa](https://mesa.readthedocs.io/latest/apis/visualization.html)). Kornhauser et al. warn that trails merge and become indistinguishable with many agents, and advise hiding all but the agents of interest ([JASSS](https://www.jasss.org/12/2/1.html)). The movement-analytics literature (Andrienko & Andrienko) finds raw trajectories and space-time cubes illegible at scale. It notes that even two trajectories are hard to read when they **share locations**, and that aggregated flow maps lose time unless they are animated or split into small multiples ([City Univ. repository](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/7614/1/Spatial_Generalization_and_Aggregation.pdf); [Andrienko et al. 2007](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/2842/1/Designing%20visual%20analytics%20methods%20for%20massive%20collections%20of%20movement%20data.pdf)). Those Andrienko claims come from search excerpts, not full text.

**Our inference.**

| Form | Good at | Bad at | Fit for 8 fixed-color agents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sprite map + action bubbles (Smallville) | Glanceable "who is where doing what now" | No history; bubbles overlap in shared buildings | Primary live view; desaturate the map |
| Trail on map | Path in spatial context | 8 agents in one small town overlap heavily | Selected agent only, fading with age |
| Visit-density heatmap | Hotspots | Loses identity and time; sequential ramp competes with agent hues | Optional toggle layer in one neutral ramp |
| Space-time cube | Co-presence as converging paths | 3D occlusion, needs rotation | Poor for a dashboard |
| Agent × time swimlane (Gantt) | No occlusion; co-location shows as vertical alignment; scales across days | Drops geography; activity needs a non-hue channel | Best history view; lanes in agent color, segments labeled by place |

The swimlane recommendation is the team's synthesis; no fetched source names it as convention for agent activity. It does share a time axis and lane order with the whisper cascade, so the two can be one linked component.

## Conversations and memory streams live in inspectors, not charts

**From the sources.** No LLM simulation paper charts memory as data. Generative Agents draws the memory stream only as **schematics**: the architecture loop, a ranked retrieval example and a reflection tree ([arXiv 2304.03442](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). Its real inspector is the per-agent persona_state text page, with sections for basic info, current action, events (`[node_N] created: description`), conversation history utterance by utterance, and thoughts with **Depth and Evidence node IDs**. It **displays no importance (poignancy) scores** anywhere ([template](https://github.com/joonspk-research/generative_agents/blob/main/environment/frontend_server/templates/persona_state/persona_state.html)). AgentSociety plays back thought and dialogue history from its database alongside an agent detail panel ([docs](https://agentsociety.readthedocs.io/en/latest/01-get-started/04-webui.html)). Humanoid Agents pairs a Unity game view with a Plotly Dash dashboard where hovering a time-series point shows the agent's emotion and activity ([arXiv 2310.05418](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05418)). Sotopia and RecAgent present conversations as **qualitative transcripts** ([Sotopia](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11667); [RecAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.02552)), and AgentSociety summarizes opinions with word clouds ([arXiv 2502.08691](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08691)). Multi-party dialogue research favors **temporal speaker-lane layouts**: TeMoCo's temporal mosaic ([Sheehan et al.](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/temoco-a-visualization-tool-for-temporal-analysis-of-multi-party-/)), Discussion Flows for turn-taking and silences ([Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.2312/evs20211060/085-089.pdf)), ConToVi's per-turn glyphs ([dbvis](https://scibib.dbvis.de/uploadedFiles/FINAL_Submission.pdf)), and Chat Circles and the Conversation Clock for turn rhythm ([Visualizing Conversation](https://www.academia.edu/125416792/Visualizing_Conversation)). Most of these dialogue-tool descriptions come from search excerpts.

**Our inference.** For conversations, the convention is a transcript: speaker names or avatars in agent color, timestamps and location. It should be **grouped by conversation (participants + place)**, not shown as one global stream. Pair it with the who-talked-when lanes above, and click a lane bar to open its transcript. Word clouds are cheap but lose who said what and when, which is exactly what a stance study needs. For memory, take the persona_state page and fill its known gaps. Show importance, recency and retrieval scores, which Generative Agents computed but never displayed. Filter by type (observation, conversation, reflection, whisper). Make Evidence IDs clickable links back to source memories. Most important for this study, link each stance reading's rationale to the specific memories it cites. A small importance-vs-time strip per agent would be a novel addition; no source supports it. The most valuable missing link across all inspectors is tracing **conversation → new memory → stance change**. No existing tool does this, and it is the causal chain this study is about.

## LLM cost: copy observability vendors, stack by call type

**From the sources.** LLM observability tools converge on a common set of views. **Time-series lines or stacked bars** show trace count, latency, tokens and cost. **KPI tiles** show totals. Ranked bars show the top tools or models. **p50/p95/p99 latency** gets its own chart. A single global group-by (model, user, feature, tag) applies across charts ([LangSmith](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/dashboards); [Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/docs/metrics/features/custom-dashboards); [Helicone mirror](https://mintlify.com/Helicone/helicone/observability/overview)). LangSmith splits cost by token type and lists Line, Stacked bar, KPI, Ranked bar, Donut and Table as its chart types. Budgets appear as limits and alerts (OpenAI project budgets under Limits), not as charted burn-downs ([JHU guide](https://support.cmts.jhu.edu/hc/en-us/articles/38383798293133-Guide-to-Managing-API-Keys-and-Usage-Limits-on-platform-openai-com)). The team found no source on cost visualization specific to multi-agent simulations.

**Our inference.** The recommended set:

- **KPI tiles** for total cost, tokens, calls, error rate and cost per sim-hour. These are the analog of NetLogo monitors.
- **Cost per sim step stacked by 3–5 call types** (plan, converse, reflect, stance-probe, embed), in a neutral scheme.
- **Per-agent cost as a ranked bar** in agent colors. Avoid an 8-layer stacked area, where only the bottom layer and the total read accurately.
- **Input vs. output tokens per step**, which exposes prompt growth as memories accumulate.
- **Cumulative spend against a horizontal budget line.** This is a sensible addition, not a copied convention.

Pitfalls to avoid: dollars and tokens on twin axes, 8-slice donuts, and mixing wall-clock and sim-time x-axes. Pick one time base, label it, and keep it consistent with the map and timelines.

## Conclusion

Most of the conventions found here are population conventions. Densities, CCDFs, alluvials, flow maps and simulated-vs-real aggregate panels exist because individual agents are unreadable at scale. Eight agents reverses that. Every agent is legible, so the dashboard's job shifts from summarizing a crowd to **tracing individual causal chains**: whisper received from whom, then conversation, then memory, then a rationale citing that memory, then a stance change. None of the surveyed tools links these steps across views, and Generative Agents, the closest precedent, has no time axis at all. The opportunity lies in a shared sim-time axis and lane order across the stance chart, the cascade swimlane and the activity swimlane, with clicks that open the cited memories and transcripts.

The color rule is a precondition for that linking. It only works if the reader can trust that a color always means the same agent. Borrowing Chuang's stance-colored lines or Project Sid's red/blue sentiment edges would break that trust. The remaining open questions are empirical: whether LLM 0–100 scores are effectively coarse, and whether 8 lanes in three linked swimlanes stay readable over multi-day runs. A first prototype should test both.
